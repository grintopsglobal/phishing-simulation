Know the signs of phishing

A phishing email rarely has one obvious giveaway. Usually several small signs show up together. Open each card to see an example and what you should do.

  1. A sender address that is almost right

    Fake domains and typosquatting

    The sender name can be set to anything, so what you need to read is the email address, especially the part after the @ sign. Scammers create domains that look very close to the real one: swapping a letter, adding words like "secure" or "support", or using a different ending.

    What to do

    Tap the sender name to see the full address. Read the domain letter by letter. If it differs from the one you usually see, don't respond and report it to the IT team.

    Example: payroll email
    Realpayroll@contohsejahtera.co.id
    Fakepayroll@contohsejahtera-mail.com
    Fakepayroll@contohsejahtrea.co.id("e" and "r" swapped)
    Fakepayroll@contohsejahtera.co.ld(letter L, not I)

  2. Pressure and threats

    Designed to make you panic

    "Account blocked in 2 hours", "fine if not paid now", "last day today". Panic makes us act before we think, and that is exactly what scammers want. Curiosity bait works the same way, for example "salary list for all employees".

    What to do

    Pause. Official organisations rarely give deadlines of a few hours by email. Check whether it is true through the official app or phone number, not through the email.

    Example subject lines
    URGENT!!! Your account will be blocked in 2 hours
    Final warning: late payment penalty
    Confidential: 2027 salary revision (do not share)

  3. Link text that differs from the real address

    What you see is not always where you go

    The text of a link can say anything, including an official website address. The real destination only appears when you hover over the link, usually in the bottom left corner of the browser. What decides the destination is the domain right before the first slash.

    What to do

    On a computer, hover without clicking. On a phone, long-press the link to see the address. If in doubt, type the official website address yourself.

  4. Attachments you did not ask for

    Files in disguise

    Attachments can contain malware. Be careful with files you were not expecting, especially ones ending in .exe, .zip, .html, or Office files that ask you to "Enable Content". File names can be disguised, for example .pdf.exe, which is really a program.

    What to do

    Don't open unexpected attachments, even from a colleague or a supplier you know. Confirm by chat or phone, and report anything suspicious to the IT team.

    Example attachment names
    Invoice_Okt2026.pdf.exeProgram
    Surat_Teguran.zipArchive
    Laporan_Q3.xlsmHas macros
    Formulir_Verifikasi.htmlWeb page

  5. Requests for your password, PIN, or OTP

    Sensitive data and credentials

    Banks, tax offices, couriers, and the IT team never ask for your password, PIN, OTP code, or card CVV by email, chat, or phone. Fake sign-in pages are often made to look almost identical to the real ones.

    What to do

    Never give your password or OTP to anyone. If you already typed it into a suspicious page, change your password right away on the official site and report it to the IT team.

    Example form on a fake page
    Work email Password OTP code from SMS

    Danger Asks for a password and an OTP at the same time.

  6. A generic greeting instead of your name

    Generic greetings

    Phishing is usually sent to thousands of addresses at once, so the scammer does not know your name. Services you use usually do. A generic greeting is not proof on its own, but it is a reason to look more closely.

    What to do

    If the greeting is generic and the email asks for something, check the other signs: the sender address, the links, and what is being requested.

    Example greetings
    Be careful"Dear Customer,"
    Be careful"Dear Employee,"
    Be careful"Dear Winner,"
    More normalGreets you by name

  7. Odd language and typos

    Never went through an official review

    Official communication is usually checked before it is sent. Typos, mixed languages, random capital letters, and too many exclamation marks often appear in phishing emails. Some scammers write carefully, so look at this sign together with the others.

    What to do

    Read the email calmly. If the writing style is not what you normally get from that sender, treat it as a reason to check further.

    Example sentence

    Dear costumer, we have detected suspicous activity on you account. kindly verify now to avoid it get block!!!

  8. Urgent money transfer requests

    BEC, or scams impersonating your manager

    Scammers pose as a manager, director, or supplier and ask for a quick transfer or a change of bank details. The pattern: a new number or address, a reason why they are busy, a request for secrecy, and "don't call me".

    What to do

    Verify by phone, on the number you already have saved, not the one in the message. Follow the finance approval procedure, however urgent the request sounds.

    Example chat
    The Director (new number)
    I'm in a meeting. Please transfer Rp48,500,000 to this supplier's account now.
    Don't call me yet, I'll explain later. Keep this from the team for now.

So what does a safe email look like?

Most of the emails you get every day are real. Being alert does not mean suspecting every email. A safe email usually meets all three of these at once:

The sender matches

The domain is exactly the one you normally receive, for example your own company's domain.

The context makes sense

It relates to something you actually did or are expecting, like an order or a meeting.

It asks for no secrets

It does not ask for a password, OTP, or transfer, and its links go to an official domain.
